# Beware, Activity and Productivity are eerily similar. 
My co-founder and I united with the idea to extend human capabilities in jobs that keep the world running: manufacturing, electricity, utilities etc. It's been 3 weeks, and I have lost conviction in the market.

Neither of us are from this industry (I just graduated this year, and him, the year before), the idea was ambitious to say the least. We managed to get on call with close to 20 calls with people in automotive, food, semiconductor fabs, contract technicians etc, in a span of 2 and a half weeks in an industry neither of us have any right to demand people's time in. That sounds like quite a productive thing to do. But that alone doesn't tell the full story.

Throughout the journey, I had this constant sense of confusion. I kept telling myself that's just how the process is. We are trying to build a big time tech company, one call at a time... it's bound to feel confusing. On the other hand, I noticed that I am not being as resourceful as I usually can be. I felt like I was far more resourceful when I was a software dev intern working at a small startup. This realization was causing a lot of turmoil within me, because I felt like I had "lost" a certain ability that I once used to take pride in. I was fine with it though, because my cofounder was able to get us calls and we kept gaining more knowledge about the industry. 

Cut to 2.5 weeks and a rough couple of days. I now know why we felt that way, and why it took 2 weeks to even get here. It started from us constantly hearing "talk to your users". But that's conventional startup wisdom, yes, but it's one that is very easily miscommunicated. 

A useful way to think of startups is to consider a startup to be a research lab forming a bunch of (serial/parallel) hypothesis and running experiments to resolve each of them into some insight that can dictate their next course of action (which could be, forming more hypothesis) to eventually end up with an impactful outcome for the world. Some people like to call them bets, some call them ideas, or maybe even hunches, but I find that calling them hypothesis and subjecting it to the scientific method sits well with my model of the world. 

What I realized in our journey is that, before every conversation, you have the mammoth task of "build a company" in front of you and you are not sure what you actually want to understand from the work you are doing this week, which in our case, was discovery calls. If you segment this large task of company building, and convert it to a sequential list of hypothesis to test / update (the most critical ones invalidated as soon as possible), then gaining clarity and being resourceful while you're at it gets a lot easier. 

Each conversation, in theory, can give you an insight with which you can modify or create new hypothesis. Moving through these uncertainities fast and using that to form decisions on what to do next, is what is producitivity for startups. We were collecting a lot of facts, we understood more and more about the industry. But this new knowledge didn't progress our action meaningfully, it didn't reduce the complexity of the action space available to us. Generating insights is part of productivity, but without the nuance of "generate insights to clear your hypothesis", we only managed to emulate what a productive startup does witout intending to at all. 

This is a very scary trap. But, we can avoid this by maintaining state. Think of the startup as a state machine, take the current hypothesis and internal state as input, run it through relevant experiments, and update the internal state according to the output. Simply being clear about what we know, what we don't know, and what we need to know to take the next step is how you can side step this scary trap. Then everything you do within the scope of these experiments: research, calls, MVPs, pilots, all make a lot of sense and provide a condusive environment for maximum productivity. This constrainted problem space enables you to become "relentlessly resourceful" within that set scope. 

Understanding/discovery calls are still very important when you don't know what questions matter. At some point during the discovery process, you are gonna form intuitions about how the product should look and what the market is like. At that point, it's important to switch gears into the state machine. 

The funny thing is, we hoped to reach this clarity through lots of conversations. In a way, this realization has been through these calls but not because they went amazingly well or that we found our product. Just the frustration that conventional wisdom is failing me (it wasn't really) is what got me out of that and into thinking these things through. "talk to users" is a good idea, not because you immediately get this wisdom, but you are likely to converge onto this if you go down the path of talking to more people. If you never talk to users, your chances of stumbling upon this understanding is very low.
